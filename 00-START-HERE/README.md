# 00 — Start Here

You are standing at the entrance of the whole curriculum. This short orientation exists so the
rest of it works for you: how the repository is organized, how programming actually works, how to
study material that is meant to last, and how to plan the time it takes.

## What this subject covers

- How to use this repository: navigation, exercises, solutions, progress tracking.
- What a program actually is, from a human instruction to a machine carrying it out — before any
  syntax appears.
- An evidence-based study method (retrieval, spacing, breaking examples on purpose).
- Building a realistic study plan you will actually keep.
- The prerequisite map of the entire curriculum, so you always know what depends on what.

## Why it matters

Most self-study attempts fail for two reasons: starting in the wrong place, and reading instead of
doing. Fifteen minutes here prevents both. This subject also introduces the vocabulary ("lesson",
"exercise", "completion criteria", "milestone") that every later lesson assumes.

## Prerequisites

None. This subject assumes nothing about you — no prior programming, no particular computer setup,
nothing to install yet.

## Lessons

Suggested order, each building on the previous:

- `how-to-use-this-repository.md` — navigation, how exercises and solutions are separated, the
  quality gate, and how progress is tracked.
- `how-programming-works.md` — what computers do, what a program is, and why "the computer is fast
  but literal" explains almost every beginner bug.
- `learning-method.md` — retrieval practice, spacing, and the predict/run/break loop that turns
  reading into skill.
- `study-planning.md` — turning a busy life into a sustainable weekly rhythm.
- `prerequisite-map.md` — the dependency graph of all 21 subjects, and how to place yourself in it.
- `frequently-asked-questions.md` — the questions every beginner asks in week one, answered once.

## Exercises and practice

Orientation exercises are reflective rather than technical — they are still mandatory:

1. Write your personal "why" for learning this, and the date you would consider it achieved.
2. Build your weekly study schedule in `study-planning.md` and put the sessions in your calendar.
3. Fill the first rows of your copy of [PROGRESS-TRACKER.md](../PROGRESS-TRACKER.md) — the act of
   starting it is the exercise.

## Expected outcomes

When you finish this subject you can:

- Explain how this repository is organized and where to go next, at any point in the curriculum.
- Describe in your own words what a program is and what "the computer does exactly what you wrote"
  means.
- Say which milestone you are on and why you have not skipped ahead.
- Run a study session that produces evidence (code run, exercise completed) rather than recognition.

## Common blockers

- **Impatience.** "I want to write code, not read about studying." The code lessons start
  immediately after this subject; the method here is what keeps you from stalling in week three.
- **Perfect planning.** A plan you follow at 70% beats a perfect plan abandoned in week two.
