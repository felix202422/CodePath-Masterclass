# Study Planning

## 1. Learning Objectives

After this lesson you will be able to:

- [ ] Build a weekly study plan sized to your real available time
- [ ] Choose a starting subject honestly, based on evidence, not ambition
- [ ] Handle a missed week without derailing the whole effort
- [ ] Measure the plan by output evidence, not hours logged

## 2. Prerequisites

- [The learning method](learning-method.md) — you can only plan sessions you know how to run.
- [How to use this repository](how-to-use-this-repository.md) — the tracker and the proficiency
  levels this plan reports into.

## 3. Introduction

A study plan has one job: **make the next session obvious, so starting costs no willpower.** Not to
cover a fixed number of pages, not to hit an arbitrary deadline — to keep the loop running for
months, which is the timescale at which this curriculum actually pays.

This lesson helps you build a plan that survives contact with a real life: a job, classes, family,
and bad weeks.

## 4. The Problem It Solves

Two classic failure shapes, and this lesson is engineered against both:

1. **The ambitious plan.** "Two hours every evening" collapses by week three, the gap feels like
   failure, and the effort is abandoned. Plans fail because they are sized for a fictional version
   of you.
2. **The destination plan.** "Finish in six months" makes progress feel permanently behind schedule,
   and the psychological cost of being behind eventually outweighs the interest in the subject.

The alternative: a plan sized for your *worst* realistic week, measured by evidence produced, and
evaluated monthly rather than daily.

## 5. Core Concepts

### 5.1 Size the plan at 70%

Ask how much time you have in a **bad** week — not a good one. Multiply by 0.7 and use that number.
It sounds conservative; it is the only version that survives exams, travel, illness, and bad
motivation. An under-promised plan you execute beats an over-promised plan you abandon, and the
psychological compounding of "I did what I said" is the single biggest predictor of finishing.

### 5.2 The minimum viable week

Whatever your real number is, protect three components:

| Component | Minimum | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One deep session | 60–90 min | Lessons and exercises need uninterrupted focus |
| One retrieval session | 30 min | Self-tests and spaced returns (Drill 2 and 4) |
| One project block | 60+ min | Integration — where fragments become skill |

If a week can only hold 3 hours total, that is a real plan. Two years of 3-hour weeks produces a
different career than three months of heroic weeks followed by silence.

### 5.3 Sequence by prerequisite, not by interest

The temptation is to study what is exciting (React, games, AI) and postpone what is dull (databases,
Git). The prerequisite structure exists to defeat exactly this: the exciting things are *built from*
the dull things, and studying them out of order guarantees you will stall at the first real
challenge. [prerequisite-map.md](prerequisite-map.md) shows the dependency graph; follow it.

Exception handling, done honestly: if you have professional experience in a subject, test out of it
(cold knowledge checks and one exercise set) and mark your real level in the tracker. Skipping
*after demonstrated competence* is strategy; skipping *before* is borrowing against a debt with
interest.

### 5.4 Plan in outputs, not hours

Hours are inputs and lie comfortably. Outputs cannot lie:

- ✅ "Complete `loops.md` exercises 1–4 with criteria met" — an output.
- ✅ "Write the branch-conflict exercise from memory" — an output.
- ❌ "Study for 2 hours" — an input that a distracted evening happily satisfies.

Write each week's plan as 2–4 outputs. If you finish them, the week is a success regardless of how
long it took; if you do not, no number of hours logged changes that.

### 5.5 The missed-week protocol

You will miss a week. Plan the return in advance:

1. **Do not restart.** Restarting from subject 0 is a form of procrastination.
2. Open the tracker, find the last evidence entry, and do a **cold self-test** of that lesson.
3. Whatever you forgot goes in the gap column; redo those exercises (10–20 minutes).
4. Resume where the evidence says you are, not where guilt says you should be.

Missed weeks are data about your plan's size, not character flaws. If you miss two in a month, the
plan is too big — shrink it rather than shrinking yourself.

## 6. How It Works — building the plan, step by step

1. **Audit time.** For one normal week, write down every free block. Be brutal about what counts
   (the commute with headphones counts; the aspirational 6 a.m. does not).
2. **Pick the number.** 70% of the audit. That is your weekly budget.
3. **Place the three components** in fixed slots where possible — same time, same place. Fixed
   slots remove the daily negotiation.
4. **Write 2–4 weekly outputs** from the current subject's lesson and exercise list.
5. **Schedule the spaced returns** for last week's lessons inside this week's sessions.
6. **Review monthly** (30 minutes): update the tracker, count outputs produced, adjust the budget
   up or down honestly.

Example month at five hours a week:

```text
Week 1  Deep: variables-and-data-types + exercises 1–2 | Retrieval: self-test | Project: none (orientation)
Week 2  Deep: operators + conditionals + exercises    | Retrieval: +2-day redo | Project: none
Week 3  Deep: loops + exercises                      | Retrieval: cold test  | Project: first CLI sketch
Week 4  Deep: loops exercises 3–4, functions         | Retrieval: 2 lessons  | Project: CLI sketch continued
        Monthly review: what stuck, what slipped, next month's focus
```

Note the shape: one concept area per deep session, retrieval always present, project always moving,
and a monthly review that is the only planning session — daily planning is waste.

## 7. Real-World Applications

This is how the profession manages long work. Teams plan in small increments with explicit outputs,
review at fixed intervals, and adjust based on evidence rather than guilt. Learning to run your own
education this way transfers directly to running projects — and to explaining your progress
credibly, which is what interviews actually test.

## 8. Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it happens | How to prevent it |
|---|---|---|
| Planning from an ideal week | Aspirational bias | Plan from last month's *actual* calendar |
| Scheduling daily progress checks | Motivation seeking | Daily: do the session. Monthly: judge progress |
| Studying only on weekends | Weekday overload | Two weekday micro-sessions beat one long weekend session you resent |
| Rewriting the plan constantly | Planning is easier than doing | Fixed monthly review; between reviews, only the missed-week protocol |
| Feeling behind a calendar goal | Deadline framing | The roadmap gates on competence; delete the deadline, keep the gate |

## 9. Best Practices

- **Anchor sessions to an existing habit** ("after dinner, at the same table") — cues beat resolve.
- **Prepare the next session before stopping** (leave an exercise half-attempted): starting is then
  a continuation, not a cold start.
- **Keep the plan in one place** — the tracker template has a monthly review section; use it.
- **Tell someone your weekly outputs.** External commitment is the cheapest accountability there is.

## 10. Limitations and Trade-Offs

A plan cannot create time that does not exist. If the honest audit yields two hours a week, the
curriculum takes years — that is a real fact to accept early, not a failure to discover late. Two
hours a week for years still produces a professional; abandoning the plan in month two produces
nothing. If your budget is truly small, the correct move is to reduce scope (fewer projects, deeper
core) rather than to pretend otherwise.

## 11. Practical Exercises

### Introductory

1. **Run the time audit.** Write down every free block of a normal week.
   **Completion criteria:** a written list with realistic totals, including obligations you usually
   forget.
2. **Compute your budget.** 70% of the audit, rounded down. **Completion criteria:** a number, in
   writing, with the three components assigned to named slots.

### Intermediate

3. **Write next week's plan as outputs.** Pull 2–4 outputs from your current subject's lesson list.
   **Completion criteria:** every line is testable ("exercises 1–4 with criteria met"), none is
   "study X".
4. **Script your missed week.** Write exactly what you will do the week after a missed one.
   **Completion criteria:** a written 3-step protocol including the cold self-test.

### Independent problem-solving

5. **Design your own accountability system** that works without other people: a public repo, a
   weekly log, a streak record — whatever you will actually maintain. It must produce evidence a
   stranger could inspect. **Completion criteria:** the system exists, has produced one week of
   records, and you can state what would make you abandon it (then design against that).

## 12. Knowledge Check

1. Why does sizing a plan at 70% of a *bad* week outperform sizing it from your best week?
2. What is the difference between "study for 2 hours" and an output-based plan line? Give an
   example of each for the same lesson.
3. You missed a week. What are the first three things you do, in order?
4. When is skipping a subject legitimate, and what evidence is required first?

## 13. Completion Criteria

- [ ] You have a written weekly budget with three named session slots
- [ ] Next week's plan exists as 2–4 testable outputs
- [ ] Your missed-week protocol is written down before you need it
- [ ] Your first monthly review is in the calendar

## 14. Summary

- Plan from your worst realistic week at 70%, not from ambition.
- Protect one deep session, one retrieval session, one project block.
- Plan outputs, never hours; judge progress by evidence at monthly review.
- Missed weeks trigger a protocol, not a restart.
- Follow the prerequisite order; test out of subjects only with demonstrated competence.
